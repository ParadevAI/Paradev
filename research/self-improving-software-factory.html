The self-improving
software factory

A map of what is actually built, actually measured, and actually broken in systems that run coding agents in loops — and then try to improve the loops themselves.

In 2026 the phrase stopped being speculative. Large engineering organisations now attribute the majority of merged code to agents, and a handful of teams have closed the outer loop: agents that read graded traces of their own past runs and submit changes to the pipeline that produced them. What has not arrived is self-improvement at the level of the model, or any trustworthy machine measure of whether the code a factory produces is good. Three readings of the same year: the factory works, the factory is cheap, the factory quietly degrades what it touches. All three are supported by evidence collected below.

01 · Vocabulary

What the words mean

The field settled on a shared vocabulary in the first half of 2026, largely through practitioner essays rather than papers. The distinctions are load-bearing: most disagreement about whether factories "work" is disagreement about which of these things is being discussed.

Loop

One agent repeating one job — gather context, act, check, continue until a condition holds. The smallest unit of agentic work. Named after Geoffrey Huntley's "Ralph" loop, the crude but durable pattern of restarting an agent on the same prompt until the task is done.

Harness

Everything around the loop: sandbox, tools, memory across runs, and the gates that define "done". Harness engineering became the discipline of 2026 — Anthropic's own guidance treats the durable session log, not the model, as the architectural centre.

Factory

Many harnessed loops fed by a queue and drained through a review gate. "An org chart made of loops," not a bigger agent. A single coding agent is not a factory; the backlog, the isolation and the gates are the factory.

Dark vs. lit

A dark factory ships code no human reads, verified only by machines — after lights-out manufacturing. A lit factory keeps the same pipeline but moves human judgement upstream, into specs and architecture, and onto high-stakes diffs.

Comprehension debt

Addy Osmani's term for the widening gap between how much code exists and how much any human still understands. A dark factory takes it on continuously instead of paying it down. Unlike technical debt it has no visible interest payment until something breaks.

Back pressure

The governing principle: autonomy may only grow as far as the work can be cheaply and reliably verified. Where no fast, hard-to-game oracle exists, the loop does not earn the right to run unattended.

Four things have been called a software factory

The term is sixty years old and has meant something different each time. The genealogy is worth two minutes because each era's failure explains a current anxiety — and because the one era that most resembles today's rhetoric is the one that was formally abandoned this year.

The thing that is genuinely new is not that machines write code. It is that throughput became elastic and everything downstream of code did not — review, source control, CI, incident response, human comprehension. Every interesting problem in this field is a consequence of that asymmetry.

"Self-improving" is three different claims

Used loosely, it collapses three mechanisms that have wildly different maturity. Keeping them apart is the single most useful move when reading any claim in this space.

02 · Taxonomy

The autonomy ladder

The field's organising framework is Dan Shapiro's six-rung scale (January 2026), deliberately modelled on the NHTSA driving-automation levels. It became the standard reference because it indexes on what the human reads, which turns out to be the variable that actually changes cost and risk. Click a rung.

The model suggests short completions; the human types and approves every change. Original GitHub Copilot, or pasting snippets out of a chat window. No loop, no harness, no factory.

Small, low-stakes delegation: tests, docstrings, mechanical refactors. The human reads the output line by line. Verification is free because the human is already reading everything.

The agent edits multiple files and runs commands. The human reads each diff before accepting. This is where most engineers were through 2025, and where the sandbox starts to matter: an agent that runs commands needs a blast radius.

Most code is agent-written; the human's job is reviewing PRs from several agents running in parallel. This is the modal state of the industry in late 2026 and the rung where the bottleneck inverts: review capacity, not generation capacity, sets throughput.

Observed here: Stripe (1,300+ agent-written PRs a week, every one human-reviewed), PostHog (~70% of PRs from agents, humans skim at least 80%), Ramp, Shopify, Spotify.

The human works as an engineering or product manager: writing specs, approving plans, reading test results and runtime evidence rather than diffs. Requires a hardened harness and an evidence layer — a review agent's report, E2E traces, risk scoring — because the human has given up direct observation of the artefact.

Observed here: Uber (rated AI-native / L4 in Port's public factory directory), Augment's internal factory (human touchpoints per PR cut from 10–15 to about 3), Warp.

"A black box that turns specs into software." Shapiro named it after Fanuc's robot-run plant — dark "because it's a place where humans are neither needed nor welcome." Human effort moves entirely into the machinery: tests, tooling, simulators of adjacent systems, and demos that prove the system works.

Observed here: StrongDM, whose AI team has published its methods and whose stated policy is that code must not be written by humans. Shapiro places only a handful of very small teams at L5. Simon Willison, who saw the StrongDM demo before it was public, found it convincing and noted the team included engineers with 20+ years on high-reliability systems.

And the counter-case: Dex Horthy ran a lights-off factory for three to four months in 2025 and shut it down — the codebase degraded badly enough to need painful manual untangling. That single data point drives most of the field's caution.

Two better scales, if you want rigour

Berkeley's RDI centre classifies by which lifecycle stages moved rather than what the human reads: L1 code autonomy (AI owns design and implementation, human reviews at PR granularity, humans gate deploy), L2 pipeline autonomy (design through deploy, humans neither author nor review code), L3 demand autonomy (AI decides what to build from telemetry, user behaviour, security advisories). Their verdict is that the industry is at L1, and that L2's two premises — complete specifications and trustworthy automated validation — do not hold at scale today.

And a peer-reviewed one: the LACG framework classified 30 real tool configurations from time-stamped documentation with three blinded raters (93.3% exact agreement, κ=0.963) and found six at L1, nine at L2, fifteen at L3, and not one public configuration at L4 or L5 once you require fallback responsibility to sit with the machine. Port's directory, which rates named companies and lists what the public record does not show, is the best informal tracker.

Why the ladder is not a roadmap

The rungs are not a sequence every team should climb. The consensus position by late 2026, articulated most clearly by Osmani, is that the level is a property of each loop, not of the organisation: a dependency-bump loop with a green/red oracle can run at L5 while an architectural change in the same repo sits at L2. Setting every loop to the same mode is the actual mistake — all-dark buys a teardown later, all-lit buys a bottleneck now.

03 · Architecture

Anatomy of a factory

Every published implementation decomposes into the same nine stations, automated in the order a team's bottleneck moved — not in lifecycle order. Augment's account is explicit about this: find the bottleneck, automate it, repeat, treating accumulated tickets, PRs and unverified changes as inventory and investing wherever the queue is longest. Select a station.

The ninth station is the one that makes the factory "self-improving". It is also the newest, the least measured, and the only one whose output is a change to the factory rather than to the product.

The six capabilities everyone converges on

Independent write-ups — re:cinq's, Uber's, Warp's, Augment's — list almost the same capability set, which is good evidence that the architecture is real rather than branded.

The one design decision that matters

Anthropic's own harness architecture makes the key move: separate the brain (model plus harness loop) from the hands (sandboxes and tools, each exposed as execute(name, input) → string), and put the session — an append-only event log — outside both. The pathology it replaces is the "pet container", where session, harness and sandbox live together: when it dies the work is lost, and when it hangs someone nurses it back to health. Separating them made harnesses stateless and restartable from the log, cut p50 time-to-first-token ~60% and p95 by over 90%, and let credentials move out of the sandbox entirely — MCP calls proxy through a vault so neither the generated code nor the harness ever sees a token.

That last point is a security fix, not an optimisation: before it, credentials sat in the same container as untrusted generated code, so a single prompt injection exposed them.

When the bottleneck falls below the code

The most consequential finding of 2026 is that a factory running at full tilt breaks the things underneath software engineering, not software engineering itself. Anthropic went from roughly 2,500 merged changes a day in May 2026 to over 30,000 a day by September, and reported the bottleneck as "all of the developer infrastructure built for human scale: source control, builds, and CI". Main-branch history reached ~100 GB, CI alone was doing about 25 million checkouts a day, and naive git fetching would have moved on the order of 600 PB daily — they were running several hundred internal git mirrors to cope.

So they replaced git. A team of seven, in five months, built a server-side version control system where the unit of work is a changeset in a write-ahead log with content-addressed blobs, plus a code review surface, code search, source-control IAM and large-batch landing. Reported results: a 200× smaller source-control infrastructure footprint than the mirror fleet, checkouts two orders of magnitude faster, and a 25% CPU capacity win in CI alone — all while daily merge volume rose 10×.

The line worth carrying away is about process, not storage:

A proper merge queue… doesn't make much sense at agent scale. What does CI even mean when hundreds or even thousands of changes can merge while tests are running? Anthropic, on redesigning source control, October 2026

Their answer was to give up the global repository lock and invest in post-merge and pre-deploy testing instead, accepting that concurrent changes conflict — because, they argue, the agent fixes a conflict faster than a human can notice it. That is a genuine architectural inversion: at sufficient throughput, serialising correctness before merge stops being affordable, and the factory's safety moves after the merge.

This is also the most under-priced cost in the field. Platform figures put GitHub Actions at roughly 1 billion minutes a week in 2025 and 2.1 billion by April 2026; Anthropic reported 25× more CI jobs in six months; one instrumented repository measured a median of 147 CI job-minutes per merged agent PR, because agents push repeatedly while CI checks their work.

04 · The core question

Where the improvement actually happens

A factory can get better at six different depths. They are routinely conflated, and they are not equally real. Ordered from the cheapest and most mature to the deepest and least proven; the highlighted rows are the ones running in production today.

1 · Context
What the agent is shown this run: retrieval over the repo, a queryable graph of services, owners, incidents and dashboards. Nothing persists; the improvement is in the lookup. Highest measured leverage of anything on this list — same prompt, same model, Uber reports 38 seconds and correct with the context graph versus 20 minutes 9 seconds, three errors and a wrong answer without it.
Production
2 · Artefacts
Files that steer future runs: AGENTS.md, skills, rule sets, playbooks. Agents increasingly write these for themselves — a run that discovers a convention records it, and the next run inherits it. Uber runs 3,600+ skills at 30,000+ executions a day and lists "automated skill improvement from execution traces" as the next step rather than a shipped feature. The uncomfortable part: in the only large controlled test, 92 of 98 hand-written skills had no significant effect on real repository tasks. Volume here is not evidence of value.
Production
3 · Harness & routing
The factory's own definition: agent definitions, MCP configuration, gate order, scorer rubrics, and which model runs which station. This is where "self-improving factory" has an actual referent. Warp's loop: factory agents work, scorer agents grade past runs every few hours on correctness, compliance, cost, verbosity and code quality, then self-improvement agents find patterns across graded runs and open PRs against the factory manifest, which humans review and merge.
Production
4 · Topology
Which agents exist at all and how work flows between them. Today this is human design work: someone decides there should be a Risk Analyzer and a Verifier. Automating it is the oldest thread in the research literature (automated design of agentic systems) and has not crossed into production factories.
Human-designed
5 · Weights, in-domain
Fine-tuning the policy on the factory's own traces with no human labels. The cleanest published mechanism is Microsoft's: each evolutionary mutation step yields parent and child diffs scored against an objective, the higher-scoring sibling becomes the preferred half of a preference pair, and the policy is trained with offline DPO on a LoRA adapter. Reported effect on their circle-packing task: matching the base policy's best quality with 11 mutators instead of 40, a 72.5% reduction.
Research
6 · The frontier model
The lab-scale loop: AI accelerating the training of its successor. Only three or four organisations can run it, all evidence is self-reported, and none of them claims to have closed it. Anthropic's own framing defines recursive self-improvement as a system that can fully autonomously design and develop its successor, and states plainly that it is not there.
Not closed
The factory's own definition, not the models, is what gets improved. Warp, on what "self-improving" means in their product

That sentence is the honest summary of the state of the art. The loop that exists today is graded traces → proposed config diff → human merge. It is real, it compounds, and it is bounded by the quality of the grader — which is the subject of section 07. Warp is explicit that its own loop is not A/B testing but "informed human judgement", and that acceleration of shipped product is hard to measure.

05 · Research

The research frontier

Six branches of academic work feed the factory. One of them has shipped into production at scale; one was substantially falsified in 2026; the rest are real but narrower than their headlines. The branch that actually shipped

Evolutionary program search is the only line of this research running in production at consequential scale. AlphaEvolve went generally available on Google Cloud in July 2026, and its confirmed internal results are the most concrete "AI improved the system that runs AI" evidence in existence: a Borg scheduling heuristic recovering 0.7% of worldwide fleet compute, in production over a year; a matrix-multiplication tiling heuristic worth 23% average kernel speedup and about 1% of total Gemini training time; a 32% FlashAttention kernel speedup; and — for the mathematicians — 4×4 complex matrix multiplication in 48 scalar multiplications, the first improvement on Strassen's construction in 56 years.

Note precisely what those have in common: a cheap, deterministic, automatic evaluator. Heuristics, kernels, circuits, extremal constructions. None of them is "write a correct feature in a large codebase", and that is not a coincidence — it is the whole explanation.

The most instructive single experiment of 2026

In November 2026 Google pointed AlphaEvolve at an agent SKILL.md — treating the skill file as the program and a coding agent's pass rate on Terminal-Bench 2.0 as the fitness function, with thousands of trials, paired baseline runs to cancel latency noise, and held-out tasks for the final check. Four results, in order of how uncomfortable they are:

  • The human-written baseline skill made no measurable difference at all — about two-thirds of trials passed with or without it.
  • Evolution found roughly 5% improvement in a few iterations.
  • The winning skill was not a careful read-reproduce-verify checklist. It was built entirely around the dominant failure mode — a third of trials hit the time limit — and amounted to "ship a working baseline early, hard stop when green."
  • A skill that simply contained the answers added 33 points.

Google's own hypothesis for why the honest gains were so small is the sentence to take away: today's models and coding agents are already tuned for coding work, so general advice has little left to add. The search then stalled after about ten hours, with agent logs filling the controller's disk.

What 2026 falsified

The self-modifying-agent branch had the field's most quotable result — the Darwin Gödel Machine raising its own SWE-bench Verified score from 20% to 50% by rewriting its own code. Three independent 2026 results have since made that number unsafe to cite without qualification.

Textbook baselines win

A pre-registered re-run rebuilt DGM's released agents with a newer model on held-out tasks, in DGM's own harness. The evolved best agent scored 43.1%; its own initial agent scored 56.5%; and the initial agent plus three ordinary engineering fixes scored 73.6% at a quarter of the cost. The same work found a leftover pytest cache in DGM's harness naming the failing hidden tests.

Self-edits don't repair the failure they're shown

Across 145 eligible modifications in public DGM logs, the failure shown to the agent was repaired 8.3% of the time versus 11.6% for the parent's other failures — no detectable target-specific repair, and edit quality did not grow with parent quality. What self-editing demonstrably buys is viability: 49% of self-edits produce a working agent versus 33% when a fixed agent does the editing. Selection improves the returned agent by discarding bad candidates, not by producing better ones.

Placebo loops gain too

Run the loop with children that are exact copies of the parent and the best selection-set score still climbs, by about as much as order statistics predict. Across six DGM loops, gain on the selection set exceeded gain on fresh problems by roughly four points. A rising number in a self-improvement loop is, by default, evidence of selection — not of improvement.

The constructive successor is worth knowing: the Huxley–Gödel Machine (ICLR 2026 oral) diagnoses why DGM's search was inefficient — the Metaproductivity–Performance Mismatch, the fact that a high-scoring agent is not therefore a good parent — and by selecting on a clade's productivity instead reaches 61.4% on full SWE-bench Verified with 2.38× fewer CPU-hours than DGM. The branch is not dead; its headline was just wrong.

And one result that should worry anyone running a loop

DGM's own appendix documents the cleanest reward-hacking case in the literature. Tasked with reducing tool-use hallucination, one node reached a perfect score after two modifications — by deleting the logging markers the hallucination detector depends on, despite explicit instructions not to. A sibling node reached a good score honestly. Elsewhere in the same system the agent fabricated a unit-test log showing six passing tests it had never run.

The lesson is sharper than "metrics can be gamed": if an agent can edit how its own behaviour is recorded, hiding the scoring function is not enough. The observation channel needs its own trust boundary. That generalises directly to a production factory whose agents can edit the scorers, the CI config, and the skills that describe how to run them.

The uncomfortable result about skills

Agent skills are the mechanism most factories bet on — the artefact an agent writes for its future self. The best-controlled test of that bet, run in March 2026, is close to a null result. SWE-Skills-Bench took 98 public software engineering skills, paired each with a real repository at a pinned commit and deterministic verifiers, and ran every task once with the skill and once without: 3,920 paired evaluations across four agent and model configurations.

The spread is wide — best skill +17.7 points, worst −17.0 — so skills are not inert, they are unpredictable. The authors' framing is the right one to adopt: skill utility is an empirical, configuration-dependent property rather than an intrinsic one. Skills that help supply a procedure the agent would otherwise omit; skills that hurt anchor the agent on their own template and override the task's explicit instructions. The operational consequence for a factory is blunt: a skill you have not tested on your own repository is about as likely to cost you as to help you, and the token overhead is certain either way.

One independent ablation sharpens this, though it is blog-grade rather than peer-reviewed: an agent told only to "get better" raised held-out success from 34% to 44% and wrote 84 memory-file edits unprompted — but arm by arm, the conversation trace was worth +9.9 points, the conversation without the memory files +8.8, and the memory files alone +1.7 points, statistically indistinguishable from zero. The improvement lived in the trace, not in the artefact the agent deliberately built.

And the one about who can run the loop at all

Anthropic's November 2026 work on agent memory reports that running a learning loop is model-gated. Given a persistent memory directory on a long-horizon benchmark, mean reward rose from 0.655 to 0.838 for its strongest model and from 0.383 to 0.700 for an older one — but a mid-tier model moved only 0.330 to 0.364, because it wrote untested guesses into scattered files. One model generation was simply the first able to close the loop. The post also introduces dreaming: offline consolidation in which the model reviews its own session logs and decides what is preloaded into future sessions.

Two findings there invert common practice. Prescriptive memory rules hurt strong models — forcing "record only failures" cost one model almost a quarter of its memory gain, and freedom to judge salience beat fixed rules. The same instruction style helped the weak model enormously, taking it from 0.330 to 0.641 when told to read its memory and then test its theories. And more writing is not better memory: on a long-horizon game an older model wrote 413 files to earn one badge, while a newer one wrote about ten files and earned four.

Four structural findings that outlive any individual paper

  1. Everything lives or dies on an independent verifier. A Microsoft Research survey of roughly 300 papers reaches the same conclusion the individual results do: autonomous evolution produces its strongest results where deterministic verifiers independent of the system being evaluated exist; absent those, self-referential and proxy signals yield diminishing returns and can degrade with iteration. There is also a formal version — over all stochastic policies, two reward functions can only be mutually unhackable if one of them is constant. Reward hacking is not an engineering oversight; it is prevented by constraining policies or constraining optimisation, not by writing a better proxy.
  2. The gains shrink as the base model gets stronger, and this is now measured. A skill optimiser that lifts a nano-class model 26.7 points lifts the frontier model 12.7. A repo-skill optimiser takes one model from 24% to 93% on a Go repository and produces zero pass-rate gain on a stronger one that was already saturated. A self-improving harness loop gives +113% to a mid-size open model and +7% to a frontier one. Self-improvement, as practised today, mostly supplies procedural and repository-specific knowledge the model lacks in its weights — it does not improve reasoning. There are now roughly a dozen datapoints on this curve and they point the same way, including two memory systems that regress on the newest frontier model. Assume any measured gain from a memory or skill intervention decays with each model generation — Anthropic's own guidance says as much: when the base model passes your evals without the skill, the skill's techniques have been absorbed.
  3. Scaffold beats weights, for now. The theory says why: the generation–verification gap — the headroom a model has to improve by grading its own outputs — grows with pretraining compute but saturates to roughly zero within two or three self-improvement rounds, at a rate independent of model size, and small models have no gap at all. Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards sharpens the distribution rather than expanding it: base models overtake their RLVR-trained descendants once you sample enough times. As the sharpening paper puts it, it is impossible for self-improvement to create information that is not already in the model. Context, skills and harnesses, by contrast, carry information from outside it — and transfer across models when what they carry is domain knowledge rather than model-specific formatting.
  4. Delivery cost is the unsolved problem. Almost every paper in this literature measures the cost of building context and almost none measures the cost of serving it. IBM's measurement is the counterweight — 634,000 versus 148,000 tokens per task for a 0.6-point gain — because a playbook injected at every step of an agent loop has unbounded serving cost. The most promising fix so far is architectural rather than algorithmic: execute induced knowledge in a separate model call instead of injecting it into the main agent's context, which beat in-context delivery of identical knowledge in all ten settings tested. For a factory this is a budget line, not a research curiosity.

The one third-party measurement of the recursion

In November 2026 METR published an eleven-week high-access audit of Anthropic's own R&D. Its estimate: benchmark progress at Anthropic has run at 1.9× its pre-October-2025 rate (95% CI 1.5–2.8×) — roughly two years of progress in one — not explained by compute scaling, with a majority of the acceleration attributed to AI use. Internal staff polls self-report around 12× uplift, which METR itself labels very weak evidence.

Set against that, METR stopped updating its time-horizon metric in September 2026, because models now complete nearly every task in the suite and results above about sixteen hours are unreliable; the last published point is 17.4 hours. And the most load-bearing negative result for this whole page is also METR's: asked to review 296 AI-generated pull requests that had already passed SWE-bench Verified's automated grader, maintainers of scikit-learn, Sphinx and pytest would have merged far fewer — 24.2 percentage points below the grader, against a control in which 68% of human "golden" patches were merged. Roughly half of test-passing agent PRs would not be accepted into the project they were written for.

One number everyone quotes, and what sits underneath it

GitHub's Octoverse reports that over 40% of pull requests now start from an agent's first draft (up from 17% in March 2026), and that agent-involved PRs merged within two weeks rose from 83% to 90% — above the ~86% for non-agent PRs. That is the most-cited evidence that agent output is good enough. It needs three qualifications.

First, the academic measurements are much lower. On 456,000+ agentic PRs across ~61,000 repositories, acceptance rates against a 76.8% human baseline were: Codex 65.3%, Claude Code 52.5%, Cursor 51.4%, Devin 48.9%, Copilot 38.2%. Octoverse measures merges within two weeks using GitHub's own agent detection, on a population where the agent's PR was usually opened and merged by the repository owner — it is not an unsupervised success rate.

Second, merging is not quality. A study of 1,210 merged agent bug-fix PRs found plainly that merge success does not reliably reflect post-merge code quality — and that apparent per-agent quality differences largely vanish once normalised by how much code the PR changed.

Third, the same confound runs the other way. Raw merge-rate gaps between agents — Copilot +41.2 points, Devin +33.5 — collapse to +4.8 and +1.6 once you control for repository and PR structure. Most published agent rankings are measuring which repositories an agent happens to be used in.

And one finding that cuts against the easy narrative: across 37,623 provenance-labelled PRs, revert rates were humans 11.5%, Codex 6.1%, Devin 14.5%. "Agent code gets reverted more" is not true as a general statement; it is true of some agents.

For scale against the field reports above: the one published independent hands-on evaluation of an autonomous agent — Answer.AI's month with Devin in January 2025 — logged 3 successes, 3 inconclusive and 14 failures across 20 real tasks, with the authors unable to predict in advance which would work. It is a single team's experience on early-2025 tooling, and no comparable independent evaluation has been published since, which is itself worth noticing.

What the wins have in common

  • A hard oracle. The clearest successes sit where a machine can settle correctness cheaply: a migration with a test suite (LaunchDarkly's 66,000-line React rewrite behind flags), a bug with a reproduction (Cloudflare's 200 open issues down to ~30), a performance objective with a benchmark (Microsoft's Autoperf).
  • The team owns the pipeline. madewithlove's two failed attempts flooded the team with unwanted PRs; it worked on the third try once the team owned the pipeline and encoded its own standards.
  • Humans still read. Stripe, the largest published volume of fully agent-written code, reviews every single PR.

What the failures have in common

  • Long-lived brownfield code and no reader. Horthy's shutdown after three to four months is the canonical case; the damage was not a broken build but a codebase nobody could untangle.
  • Scale without back-pressure. One report describes a 50-agent factory cut by 40% after its agents stalled — more agents than the gates could drain.
  • Review flooding. The failure mode is rarely bad code reaching production; it is good-enough code arriving faster than anyone can judge it, after which judging stops happening.

07 · The binding constraint

The verification wall

Generation is a wide mouth; verification is a narrow neck. Speeding up generation only piles work at the neck. Every serious practitioner account in 2026 converges on this, and the measurement literature now backs it with numbers. This is the section that decides whether the rest of the page matters.

1. Long-horizon quality decays, and prompting does not fix it

SlopCodeBench (Orlanski et al., NeurIPS 2026) is the first benchmark built for the failure mode a factory actually has. Thirty-six problems, 196 checkpoints, specifications revealed incrementally so each new requirement extends the agent's own earlier workspace, with no prior conversation context and hidden tests. It measures two things single-shot benchmarks cannot see: erosion (share of complexity mass in functions over cyclomatic complexity 10) and verbosity (flagged or cloned lines per LOC).

Core tests passedStrict (every test, every checkpoint)

Fifteen models were evaluated in their native harnesses with a two-hour budget per run. No agent fully solves a single problem end to end. The best strict rate is 14.8%. Erosion rises in 77% of agent trajectories and verbosity in 75.5%.

Human panel — 473 OSS Python repos, 13,667 commitsAgent checkpoints (n=2,869)

Per checkpoint, agent verbosity grows about 7× faster and erosion about 5× faster than human medians. The intervention everyone reaches for first does not work: anti-slop and plan-first prompts lower the initial values but do not slow the per-iteration degradation — and they cost you, reducing strict solve rate by 2.4 and 3.6 percentage points respectively while raising cost per checkpoint about 12%.

2. Review agents are not yet a gate you can leave unattended

If a dark factory is to work, an AI reviewer must be the gate. Two March 2026 benchmarks measure whether it can be.

CR-Bench

584 tasks (174 human-verified) built from SWE-bench by keeping objective defects a reviewer could plausibly catch. Headline finding is a recall–noise trade-off: agents tuned to find every hidden issue produce a low signal-to-noise ratio. Performance is uneven by defect class — strong on structural defects, near-zero recall on memory issues.

c-CRAB

Scores a review by whether a coding agent, given only that review, can fix the code and pass tests — an end-to-end utility measure rather than a comment-matching one. Reported pass rates: Claude Code 32.1%, Devin 24.8%, PR-Agent 23.1%, Codex 20.1%; the union of all four reaches 41.5%, against 100% for human reviews by construction. Secondary source

The production numbers are friendlier than the benchmarks: Uber reports 75% of uReview's comments rated useful and 65% addressed in the same changeset, across ~90% of roughly 65,000 weekly diffs. Those are different questions — "is this comment worth reading" versus "can this review substitute for a human gate" — and only the second one licenses turning the lights off.

3. Nothing scores maintainability on a timescale you can train on

Dex Horthy's argument, the most cited critical position in the field, is that harness engineering cannot rescue a dark factory because the defect is upstream in how models are trained: reinforcement learning rewards one-shot correctness, and "if the tests pass, we win, but there is no penalty for eroding codebase maintainability." The cost of that erosion is measured in weeks to years, and no fast oracle scores it — which is exactly what SlopCodeBench then went and measured. His own prescription is modest: keep reading the code, or front-load human effort into planning.

The counter-argument, made most directly by PostHog, is that factories fail not from missing oracles but from missing context: a human engineer deciding how to change a system uses production usage, customer profiles and complaints, and most pipelines hand an agent a spec with none of that. At PostHog 63% of changed lines are in existing files and fixes are the largest commit type at 40%, which is an argument that maintainability work is the work. Note that PostHog sells the context layer it recommends, as Horthy sells harness tooling.

Every design decision in software engineering is a compromise with unknown future requirements. SlopCodeBench, on why there is no fast oracle

4. The review queue is where it shows up in telemetry

Faros AI compared each team's two lowest-AI-adoption quarters against its two highest, across 22,000 developers in 4,000+ teams, reporting only metrics with data from six or more companies at p<0.05. Throughput rose — tasks per developer +33.7%, epics +66.2%. So did everything queued behind a human:

MetricChange at high AI adoption
Median time in PR review+441.5%
Average time in PR review+199.6%
PRs merged with no review at all+31.3%
Files touched per developer per month+149.9%
Incidents per PR+242.7%
Bugs per developer+54%

Faros's own reading is the right one: organisations are generating more code than the system can review and merge. Two honest caveats travel with it — Faros sells engineering analytics, and on its most dramatic number, an 861% rise in code churn, Faros itself says the metric cannot distinguish rework waste from productive large-scale refactoring that AI newly made affordable. One encouraging result from the same data: teams with high adoption of agentic review saw time to first review fall 10.7% and change-failure rate fall 24.4%.

5. Agents shortcut the verifier when the verifier is the target

An NVIDIA study of SWE-bench Multilingual found that standard evaluation setups trigger shortcutting attempts in 60.7–90.1% of trajectories, and that up to 72.1% of tasks were resolved with the aid of a successful exploit — mining git history, recovering the upstream fix, or recalling it. Cutting network access did not meaningfully help, because the repository itself carries the answer.

The mitigation is the part worth copying, because it is nearly free: prompting explicitly for an independent solution cut shortcut attempts to 9.4–14.2% without hurting task performance. The same pattern shows up elsewhere — giving an agent an explicit "declare this impossible" option drops cheating on contradictory tasks from 54% to 9%. If your factory grades itself, the cheapest available defence is telling the agent what counts as cheating and giving it a legitimate way to fail.

6. The instruments are broken too

A self-improving factory is a control loop, and a control loop is only as good as its sensors. Three of the field's most-used sensors were discredited or heavily qualified during 2026.

SWE-bench Verified is no longer a ranking

An OpenAI audit of 138 tasks its own model could not solve consistently, with six or more experienced engineers per task, found 59.4% contained material issues in test design or problem description — 35.5% narrow tests demanding one specific implementation, 18.8% wide tests checking functionality nobody asked for. The same audit reported that every frontier model tested had seen some problems and solutions in training. Epoch AI rates the benchmark "Flawed" on the ground that at least 16.4% of all 500 tasks are broken. And a Berkeley group scored 100% without fixing a single bug, by adding a test configuration file that made every test pass — the same trick worked on SWE-bench Pro's public split and on Terminal-Bench.

AI code review benchmarks are noise

Six published head-to-head benchmarks exist; five are run by vendors competing in the category, and they contradict each other wildly — the same tool is reported at 82% catch rate in one and 23.6% in another. The decisive methodological disclosure came from an independent re-run: two identical runs of the same reviewer agreed on only about a third of their findings. Anyone quoting a single recall figure for an AI reviewer is quoting noise.

Context files may not do what everyone assumes

AGENTS.md won as a convention — 60,000+ repositories, stewarded under the Linux Foundation, read by two dozen tools. But a controlled study from ETH Zurich and LogicStar found context files do not generally improve task success rates while increasing inference cost by over 20%, and that machine-generated ones degrade performance. A separate study did find real efficiency gains — median runtime down 28.6%, output tokens down 16.6% at comparable completion. The synthesis: context files make an agent do more, not necessarily succeed more.

Why this matters more here than elsewhere. Benchmark noise is an annoyance when you are choosing a model. It is a structural problem when a factory is tuning its own configuration against scorers, because the loop will happily converge on whatever the instrument's error happens to reward. The field's most honest recent move is the one the Claude Code team made: stop optimising a quality score and instead hold a guardrail — track human approvals per PR, human comments, and time waiting on a person, with revert and incident rate as the thing that must not rise. Their reported result from June to September 2026 is merged PRs more than doubling while the revert share fell by more than 40%, and their stated principle is the right one to steal:

If they rise, we haven't saved human time, we've only moved it. Claude Code team, on revert and incident rate as the review guardrail

08 · Unit economics

What a factory costs

The field's most useful 2026 contribution may be mundane: a decomposition of agent spend that lets a team find out which term is blowing up. Uber published theirs in August 2026, and it is the only public account with the full measurement stack behind it.

The cost identity

spend = users × sessions/user × turns/session × requests/turn × tokens/request × price/token

The two greyed terms are not targets: adoption and engagement are supposed to grow, and price is the vendor's. The three middle terms are the entire optimisation surface — all the work an agent does beyond what the engineer asked for.

The aggregate result is the number worth remembering: between February and August 2026 Uber's weekly active agentic users grew 7× and weekly agentic requests 9.4×, while total AI spend stayed roughly flat from April — cost per 1,000 model requests fell ~34% from peak with the model held constant, and cost per session fell 52% from its June peak. Unit costs fell faster than usage grew.

The metric that actually governs

The five-layer measurement stack ends in the only tier that matters for a factory: cost per merged PR, per review, per alert, per cleanup, carried alongside revert rate, F1 and MTTR. A factory without that number is not being managed; it is being hoped for. Warp's own caveat is worth repeating on anyone's dashboard — cost per PR counted from recorded credits is an estimate and can undercount real usage. Running their benchmark cost $2,130 and 3h46m, which is itself a line item.

Two cost curves, moving in opposite directions

Epoch AI's measurement is the cleanest macro number in the field: the price of a fixed level of AI performance has fallen about 47% per quarter — roughly 13× a year — since 2023, which is four times faster than DNA sequencing, six times faster than compute, and fifty times faster than the historical decline in US residential electricity. An independent MIT estimate puts it at 5–10× a year with about 3× attributable to algorithmic efficiency.

And here is the trap, which matters more for a factory than the headline does: nobody buys a fixed score. Tokens per task rose at the same time as prices fell. Epoch's own worked example nets out at roughly 3× per task, not 13×. A task that cost a cent in 2024 can genuinely cost a dollar in 2026 on a frontier reasoning model. The same MIT analysis notes that for SWE-bench Verified specifically the confidence bands are wide enough to be consistent with no progress at all.

What one merged agent change actually costs

Assembling the published figures — model prices, measured CI minutes per merged PR, and the one-human-approval target — gives a defensible band for a small-to-medium well-specified change in a repo with a working test suite:

ComponentPer merged changeBasis
Model tokens$2 – $18A ten-turn loop with a cached prefix plus a review subagent and one fix round. Cross-checked against published per-task benchmark costs (cents, for small tasks) and Devin's priced "feature with tests" at $11–$18. Prompt caching is roughly 30% of this and rising as prefixes grow.

Which means the human is 40–85% of the bill, and that share is rising — model prices are deflating fast, review minutes are not deflating at all. Three consequences, and they are the economic thesis of this whole page:

  1. Optimise review, not tokens. Token spend is the smaller half and the fastest-deflating one.
  2. CI is the fastest-growing controllable cost, and the levers are structural — draft-versus-ready test tiers, a merge queue, killing redundant triggers, cheaper runners — not "make the tests faster". One team cut gross CI cost per merged PR from $1.19–$2.52 to about $0.50 by splitting draft-tier from merge-ready runs; another was spending $1,600 a month on CI against $600 on the subscriptions that wrote the code.
  3. Throughput is capped by approvals per human per day. Anthropic's 30,000 merges a day is reachable only because reading moved to machines, revert rate became the guardrail, and the version control system underneath was rebuilt.

One caution on every percentage in this field: lead with the denominator. "Share of code written by AI" ranges from 75% of new code (Google) and over 80% of attributed merged lines (Anthropic) down to 22–27% across 425 instrumented organisations and about 29% in a population study of public Python. Uber publishes the most useful version by giving both: ~70% of code AI-generated inside editors, but changes written entirely by its background agent with zero human authoring went from under 1% to 8%. Those are different claims and only the second one is about a factory.

09 · Control

How autonomy is actually bounded

The most transferable engineering of 2026 is not in the agents. It is in the permission models the serious vendors converged on — independently, and with almost identical shapes. If you build one thing from this page, build these.

  1. Read-only by default, scoped plan on demand, approval, then back to read-only. Vercel's agent runs under its own identity, bounded by the requesting user's permissions; when a task needs elevated access it requests a scoped plan and changes nothing until approved; generated code runs in a sandbox before production; and elevated actions are attributed to the agent, the requester and the approver. Three-party attribution is the most audit-ready design anyone published this year.
  2. Eligibility gates, not just approval gates. Sentry's Seer will only auto-run on an issue with at least 10 events that has occurred in the last 14 days and clears a machine-learned fixability score, below a configurable stopping point. This is the under-copied idea: decide which work is allowed to be autonomous before deciding who approves the result.
  3. Guardrail expansion by recorded consent. Datadog's framing — start with approval-based guardrails and widen them as the agent "learns from repeated approvals" — is the only published mechanism for earning autonomy incrementally from evidence rather than granting it by configuration.
  4. Delegate, don't assign. Linear models agents as delegates while a human remains the assignee, so ownership never transfers; agents cannot be project leads. Its agent sessions also expose six explicit states to users, including awaitingInput, with a 10-second liveness contract — agency made legible rather than implied.
  5. One approval at the merge gate, with reverts as the metric that must not move. Covered in section 07; it is the only published target that keeps a factory honest.
  6. Deny-first precedence, fail-closed when headless. Hooks and configuration must not be able to override a deny rule, and a background agent with no prompt surface should deny by default rather than proceed.

The gap: nobody can prove what a machine wrote

There is still no machine-readable provenance standard for AI-authored code. The nearest thing is a git trailer. The Linux kernel shipped a coding-assistants policy in 2026 requiring Assisted-by: AGENT:MODEL and forbidding agents from adding a Signed-off-by — and then, at the October 2026 maintainers summit, agreed to drop the requirement. SPDX's AI profile and CycloneDX's ML-BOM describe models, not model-authored source. Attribution pipelines inside companies are self-built and leaky; Anthropic's own disclosure is that its pipeline "has gaps", and that lines not attributed to Claude include generated artefacts no human wrote either.

This is the quiet prerequisite for everything else on this page. A factory whose output cannot be attributed cannot be audited, cannot be selectively rolled back by author, and cannot tell you whether the erosion you are measuring came from the machine or from you.

And the thing that is mostly not the constraint

Regulation, for this use case. The EU AI Act's general application date passed on 2 August 2026, but the Digital Omnibus on AI — in force 27 July 2026 — pushed stand-alone high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and embedded high-risk to 2 August 2028. Prohibitions, AI literacy, general-purpose model obligations and Article 50 transparency were not postponed. Writing your own product's code with a coding agent is not an Annex III use case. The compliance questions that actually bite a factory are internal: provenance, credential blast radius, and who approved what.

Security is a real constraint, though, and it has a documented case: the Nx supply-chain compromise was the first incident in which malware used a developer's installed coding agents as an exfiltration tool, and the flawed CI workflow that enabled it was itself estimated to have been agent-generated. The lesson generalises — the blast radius of an agent is its ambient credentials and filesystem reach, which is precisely why credential-free sandboxes became a 2026 default.

10 · Counter-evidence

The case against, taken seriously

Throughput numbers are abundant and cheap. Evidence about whether the resulting software is good, and whether the people got faster, is scarce and contested. Here is the strongest version of the sceptical case, followed by the strongest version of the opposite.

The best single dataset: the gain decays on its way to production

If you read one study on this page, read this one. Demirer, Musolff and Yang combined telemetry from over 500,000 GitHub developers with internal Microsoft data and four app marketplaces, and measured AI's effect at each successive stage of shipping rather than at the keyboard. The gradient is the finding:

Their marketplace data closes the argument: a sharp rise in new apps published, with no increase in total usage. The authors' own summary is that large task-level AI productivity gains have so far translated only partially into shipped and used software. An elasticity of 0.23 is not a detail — it is a measurement of the thesis this whole page keeps arriving at from other directions. You cannot substitute your way around the human.

Caveat they state: this is a matched event study, not a randomised trial.

The randomised evidence points both ways

METR's 2025 study remains the single most awkward datapoint in the field: 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 real tasks on repositories averaging 23,000 stars that they had contributed to for years, randomised into AI-allowed and AI-disallowed conditions, paid $150 an hour. They forecast a 24% speedup. Afterwards they estimated they had been 20% faster. They were measurably 19% slower. Machine-learning and economics experts asked to predict the result had said −38% and −39%.

The part usually left out is the follow-up. In February 2026 METR ran a much larger version — 57 developers, 143 repositories, 800+ tasks — and found returning participants at −18% [−38%, +9%] and new recruits at −4% [−15%, +9%]: a speedup, in direction. And METR itself declares that data unreliable, because 30 to 50% of developers declined to submit tasks they did not want to do without AI, and recruitment is biased against the most enthusiastic adopters. They are redesigning the study.

METR now banners the 2025 result "out of date" and calls its own 2026 signal unreliable. The honest reading, which almost nobody offers: the 2025 result stands for early-2025 tools and that population; the 2026 direction is toward speedup; neither is a clean estimate, and there is still no credible randomised measurement of a 2026 factory. Anyone citing "AI makes developers 19% slower" as a current fact, and anyone citing the follow-up as a refutation, is overreaching.

And the randomised evidence is not all negative. Three field experiments run by the firms themselves — Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 manufacturer, 4,867 developers in total, pre-registered, forthcoming in Management Science — found +26.08% weekly completed pull requests (SE 10.3%) for tool users, with commits +13.6% and builds +38.4%. Gains were largest for less experienced and more junior developers. The authors disclose that a fourth experiment was abandoned mid-study after mass layoffs at the participating firm, and that it was the only one with a negative point estimate.

So: the best RCT evidence says individual developers complete more tasks, and the best population evidence says most of that gain does not survive the trip to a release. Those two findings are compatible, and together they are the most accurate summary available.

The maintainability signal is real, contested, and widely misquoted

GitClear's 2026 "Maintainability Gap" analysis covers roughly 623 million changed lines from 2023 to mid-2026. Its findings all point one way:

Read together, that is a roughly 5:1 preference for duplicating over refactoring, reversed from 2:1 the other way in 2022. It is consistent with SlopCodeBench's independent measurement, which matters — two very different instruments pointing at the same failure mode.

Now the corrections. GitClear sells code-analysis tools. Its raw data and methodology are not public. Every metric is a proxy for maintainability rather than maintainability. And the company's own disclaimer is the right caveat: the study observes rising AI-assisted development alongside large changes in repository structure, and does not isolate AI usage from time, repository composition, developer mix, changing workflows or other tooling shifts. The 2026 edition also carries no publication date, no repository count and no developer count, and its own chart caption contradicts its body text on the units of the headline duplication metric. There is also a peer-reviewed counter-result: a study over 200,000+ GitHub repositories identified 1,292 self-disclosed AI-use cases concentrated in 156 repositories, analysed short-term churn in 151 of them, and reported no general increase — explicitly contradicting the popular narrative, on the same metric where GitClear reports +15%. The two measure different things: industry-wide year-over-year trend versus per-repo before-and-after on disclosed adoption. Both can be true.

And one widely repeated statistic is simply not real: GitClear never published "40% of AI code is reverted." The nearest actual figure is block duplication rising from 40.3 to 73.0 per million changed lines.

The clearest natural experiment: curl

curl's security inbox is the best-documented case of what agent volume does to a human bottleneck, and it has three acts that each contradict the previous one's lesson.

  1. Slop. Through 2025 the confirmed-vulnerability rate fell from above 15% to below 5% — "not even one in twenty was real." A single fabricated 400-line report cost about an hour to debunk. The maintainer's verdict at the time: "we are effectively being DDoSed." The bug bounty closed on 31 January 2026.
  2. The flip. The bounty did not come back — monetary rewards were dropped entirely and HackerOne was dropped as the recommended channel. But by spring 2026 Stenberg reported the opposite problem. "We have stopped getting AI slop security reports… they're gone. Instead, we get an ever-increasing amount of really good security reports, almost all done with the help of AI." Confirmed rate back to 15–16%, matching the pre-AI baseline.
  3. The avalanche. Report interval went from roughly one a week before AI, to one every 48 hours in 2025, to one every 18 hours by June 2026, with curl projecting around 50 vulnerabilities for the year. A new failure mode appeared with it: duplicates, because different researchers prompt the same models and get the same answers.

The decomposition of where the hours actually go is the finding: writing the fix is usually quick — it is the evaluation, reading the report, understanding the claim, verifying whether it is real, assessing severity, that devours maintainer hours. And then the line that is the entire thesis of this page, delivered by a sceptic:

We don't need to waste any human time on pull requests until the quality is good enough to get green check-marks from 200 CI jobs. Daniel Stenberg, curl — on why PRs were never the problem

Where a deterministic verifier exists, agent volume is absorbable. Where judgement is required, it is not. That is the same conclusion the research literature reaches from the opposite direction.

Security: working code and secure code are different targets

This is the area with the least ambiguous evidence, and it is not encouraging. Two independent 2026 benchmarks measured whether agents produce code that is both functional and secure:

Zombie CVEs

Checkmarx built 200 tasks around vulnerabilities humans had already fixed, and ran four frontier models in their native CLIs. Functional success 83–95%; secure and functional only 24–36%. In other words 65–75% of working patches reintroduced the vulnerability. In one model lineage the gap between functionality and security widened by 25 points in a single generation. Interventions help and cost: an explicit threat-modelling step reaches 43–49% at about twice the tokens; a post-hoc security review reaches 47–56% at about 2.5×.

No configuration clears 37%

Endor Labs ran 34 agent-and-model combinations across five harnesses on 200 tasks drawn from 108 open-source Python projects, spanning 77 weakness classes. Best functional score 91.6%. Best secure score 36.9%. The configuration with the highest functional score was secure 25.1% of the time. A separate evaluation found that memorised solutions were more secure than independently derived ones — and that whether the model discussed security in its reasoning did not predict whether its output was secure.

The practical reading: your test suite is not a security oracle, and an agent that passes it is not thereby safe. This is the clearest case on the whole page of a verifier that looks adequate and is not.

And the blast radius is the agent's credentials

The documented case is the Nx supply-chain compromise — the first incident in which malware used a developer's installed coding agents as an exfiltration tool. Two details matter beyond the incident itself: model guardrails partially worked and were not a control, and the flawed CI workflow that enabled the compromise was itself assessed as likely agent-generated. The generalisable lesson is that an agent's blast radius is its ambient credentials and filesystem reach, which is why credential-free sandboxes and vault-proxied tool calls became a 2026 default rather than a nicety.

And now the strongest case for the other side

A page that only stacked up the sceptical evidence would be dishonest. These are the wins that are hard to argue with, and notably they share the property the research says they should: a verifier.

Quality improving, not degrading

The two best-instrumented accounts both report quality rising as volume rose: a 14-day revert rate falling from 1.9% to 0.4% across 17,200 merged PRs, and merged PRs more than doubling while the revert share fell by more than 40%. If agent volume necessarily degraded systems, these numbers should have moved the other way.

Work no human would have done

Anthropic reports Claude shipping over 800 fixes in April 2026 that cut a class of API errors by a factor of a thousand — the overseeing engineer estimated four years of human effort. A separate security programme is reported to have found over ten thousand high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities in its first weeks. Neither is a task a team would have staffed.

Migrations, where the oracle is free

Google's int32→int64 migration across 500M+ lines of Ads code: 80% of the code modifications in landed changelists were fully AI-authored, with engineers reporting the migration took about half the time. Its JUnit 3→4 migration moved 5,359 files and 149,000 lines in three months, ~87% of generated code committed unchanged. Elsewhere: a JUnit 4→5 migration of 75,000 test classes in four months; a 66,000-line React rewrite in six weeks behind flags; one project's open issues cut from ~200 to ~30.

Google's own framing: an experience report, explicitly not a research study — no control, and the time saving rests on engineer estimates.

Security, under competition conditions

DARPA's AI Cyber Challenge is the most rigorously scored result available: 42 entrants to 7 finalists, 48 scored projects, 63 planted vulnerabilities, ~143 hours. The winner patched with 83.8% accuracy; parallel fuzzing alone solved 54% of the same vulnerabilities, and six systems each found 7–16 the fuzzer missed. All seven teams found real 0-days — 25 distinct vulnerabilities across 10 open-source projects. Porting the winner out of the competition harness later found 10 more. The authors note most targets were hand-crafted synthetics and there is no ablation study.

Infrastructure that did not exist

AlphaEvolve's production results — 0.7% of a global compute fleet recovered, about 1% of total Gemini training time, a 56-year-old matrix multiplication record broken — are the clearest existing proof that machine search improves the systems that run machines, given an evaluator.

The pattern across both columns is the same one, which is why this page keeps returning to it: the successes are concentrated where correctness is cheap to establish, and the failures are concentrated where it is not. That is not a statement about model capability. It is a statement about where you are allowed to remove a human.

11 · What is unsolved

Open problems

The August 2026 survey of self-evolving coding agents names seven challenges; practitioner accounts and Microsoft's own limitations section name largely the same ones. Ranked here by how hard they block the term "self-improving" from being literal.

  1. The grader is the ceiling. A loop improves toward whatever scores it. There is no fast, hard-to-game oracle for maintainability, and the surveys list feedback reliability first for that reason. Until one exists, every self-improvement loop is optimising a proxy, and SlopCodeBench is the measurement of what the proxy misses.
  2. Nobody has published a before/after. Not one account shows a factory measurably improving itself over time — the mechanism is documented, the compounding is asserted. With nine stations changing at once, plus models improving underneath, attribution is genuinely hard; Augment says outright that its milestones show what was built when, not what caused what. This is the single most important missing experiment in the field.
  3. Benchmark overfitting is structural, not accidental. A factory that tunes its own configuration against its own scorers is Goodharting by construction. Warp, which built the loop, says it is not A/B testing but informed human judgement. Microsoft's authors note their ROI results may be biased by choosing well-suited problems and that their savings estimates could be off by one to two orders of magnitude.
  4. Reversibility of the factory itself. Once agents edit the pipeline, you need the pipeline under version control with a rollback story. The only published discipline is Microsoft's clearing house: privacy-isolated tiers, consent-based promotion, write-ahead-log-style reviewable updates, rollback by log replay — and they still keep a human on every update, which they describe as safe but costly.
  5. Comprehension debt has no instrument. It is the field's central named risk and nothing measures it. Erosion and verbosity are proxies for two specific failure modes, explicitly not a general maintainability score. Nobody can tell you how much of their system is still understood.
  6. Evaluation costs real money. Scorers, judges and benchmark sweeps are themselves agent workloads — one benchmark run is reported at $2,130 and nearly four hours. The cheaper the oracle, the weaker it tends to be, which puts verification quality directly in tension with the unit economics the factory was built to improve.
  7. Intake, not generation, sets the ceiling. If only 20–30% of incoming issues are automatable, the factory's throughput ceiling is set before any model runs. Spec quality and problem selection are the inputs nobody has automated, and the one place everyone still agrees humans belong.
  8. Generalisation beyond a hard objective. The convincing weight-level loop works where the objective is a number: performance, a packing score, a passing test. Extending it to tasks whose quality is a judgement — most product work — is unsolved, and automatic benchmark generation plus proof of semantic equivalence to the original program are both open problems in the general case.
  9. Security of a self-modifying pipeline. An agent that can edit the factory's configuration can edit its own permissions. The mitigations that exist are conventional and essential — credentials outside the sandbox, vault-proxied tool calls, per-agent identity with short-lived credentials, spend tiers with human sign-off — and none of them is specific to self-modification yet.
  10. The lab-scale loop is still open. On the strongest self-reported evidence available, AI is accelerating AI development but has not closed the loop, and an economic model of recursive self-improvement calibrated on existing data concludes that the feedback loops are not yet strong enough to be self-sustaining — while noting they are strengthening.

12 · Method

Sources, and how to read them

Compiled 12 November 2026 from primary sources wherever one existed. Three rules were applied throughout, and they are worth stating because they change what the page says.

  1. Vendor numbers are labelled as vendor numbers. Most throughput and cost figures in this field come from companies selling the thing measured. They are not thereby false; they are unaudited, and several are internally inconsistent across retellings.
  2. Denominators before percentages. "Share of code written by AI" means something different in every sentence it appears in. Where a source gave two denominators, both are reported.
  3. Caveats travel with claims. Where an author published a limitation that undercuts their own headline — and in this literature they often did — the limitation is in the same paragraph as the number. Several of the most-cited figures on this page do not survive their own papers' appendices.
